DESIGN PROPOSAL · OCTOBER 2026

Less to read.
More to inspect.

One workspace, six kinds of process information. Keep the brief and artifact in view, then let people inspect the details they need. This page separates research findings from the design choices they inspire.

01 / VERBOSITY

Start with a short account.

“What is happening, and do I need to look closer?”

What the research says

Yu et al. (2025) compared three transparency levels in simulated poster-design agent videos with 40 participants. More transparency improved reported trust, satisfaction, and willingness to use. This was a video-based experience study, not evidence that more information improves error detection.

Our design choice

Offer Off, Status, Concise, and Detailed. Keep the process in one place rather than adding a new message for every minor action. The proposed view starts with concise commentary; users can change it during the replay.

What to investigate

Which updates do people actually inspect? Does extra commentary improve their judgments, or only their confidence?

The paper motivates comparison between displays. It does not establish this layout, these four settings, or a universally best amount of detail.

02 / THINKING

Keep explanations optional.

“Why did it take this approach?”

What the research says

Fernandes et al. (2026) compared answer-only, summary-trace, and full-trace conditions in a 559-person logic-task study. Summaries raised trust without improving performance over answer-only; the full-trace condition performed worse. That condition also used a different model and presentation order, so trace length alone is not a clean causal explanation.

Our design choice

Place a reasoning note inside the relevant step, off in the proposed default. Label it as an authored explanation and keep it visually separate from tool results. A persuasive rationale must not be presented as proof.

What to investigate

Do people consult an explanation at a decision point? Does it help them identify the four-seat pricing error?

These are written example notes, not private chain-of-thought from a running model. The study did not test this exact on-demand interface.

03 / TOOL SUMMARIES

Make an action easy to locate.

“What did it change or check?”

What the research says

Liao, Gruen, and Miller (2020) interviewed 20 UX and design practitioners and developed an XAI question bank to connect explanation design with users’ questions. It supports starting from an information need rather than exposing everything a system can produce.

Our design choice

Use short action rows: the operation, the file or target, and the result. For example: “Test · booking.test.js — 1 and 8 seats pass; 4 seats fails.” Keep unperformed checks explicitly distinguishable from passing checks.

What to investigate

Can users find whether a specific requirement was checked, and correctly explain what the result means?

Applying a question-based approach to coding-agent tool summaries is our design inference. The source did not evaluate this particular component.

04 / FULL TOOL OUTPUT

Put evidence one click away.

“What exactly did the check return?”

What the research says

Vasconcelos et al. (2023) studied simulated AI assistance in maze tasks. Their experiments show that explanations can reduce overreliance when verifying them is sufficiently easy relative to doing the task. Verification effort matters.

Our design choice

Put the exact example command and result under its step, behind a “Full tool output” disclosure. People can move from a summary to a specific result without searching a separate wall of logs. Full output can be enabled independently of summaries.

What to investigate

Does connecting a summary to its record reduce the time and effort of finding an error? Do people understand a skipped test?

A maze study does not prove that logs improve coding reviews. This is a design hypothesis. All logs in this prototype are authored fixtures, not newly executed agent tests.

05 / SUBAGENTS

Show the work behind the role.

“Who checked this, and what did they check?”

What the research says

Pareek et al. (2026) compared five multi-agent interface variants with 12 participants. Information needs depended on the task, and eight participants expressed interest in progressive disclosure. Final answers were correct, although individual agents could be wrong; this was not a test of detecting incorrect final outputs.

Our design choice

Show a reviewer card within the relevant step. Summary shows its role and returned finding. Full adds the task, inputs, scope, and handoff. Off removes attribution and the delegated-work card; independently enabled tool records can remain visible.

What to investigate

Do users judge the scope of the reviewer’s checks, or assume that an additional agent makes the whole artifact trustworthy?

A second agent is not a quality guarantee. The paper’s five variants also changed interaction and aggregation mechanisms, not only information quantity.

06 / ARTIFACT VERSIONS

Let people inspect a change.

“What is different now?”

What the research says

Wu, Terry, and Cai (2022) introduced AI Chains, where people can inspect and modify intermediate results in multi-step LLM workflows. Their 20-person study reported improved task outcomes, transparency, controllability, and collaboration relative to a non-chaining baseline.

Our design choice

Keep historical versions beside the artifact preview. Try the actual behavior in v2 and v3, or open a small comparison explaining the changed discount condition. Off hides version information; Latest shows the current artifact; All allows historical inspection.

What to investigate

Can users connect a change in the process to a visible or functional change in the artifact?

AI Chains combined visibility with editing and chaining; it did not isolate version history. Our version selector is an interaction proposal, not a replicated research result.

BOUNDARIES

One proposal.
One shared workspace.

The artifact should help us ask better research questions.

Where the six layers come from

The category names follow the public Agent Veil playground as inspected on October 27, 2026. Its six information categories differ from its six preset rungs. This proposal makes each category independently adjustable; the preset mappings for subagents and versions are implementation choices to discuss.

What stays constant

Changing visibility does not rerun the agent or change its final artifact. The preset task, authored events, and final version are fixed. Deliberately selecting an earlier artifact version shows that earlier state. The four-seat pricing error belongs to the intermediate v2 artifact and is fixed in v3.

What works here

Booking calculations and validation, local demo confirmation, visibility controls, step disclosures, delegated-work views, version comparison, responsive preview, and a scripted replay. No API keys, live AI, payment, analytics, session uploads, or participant-data storage.

What is still a team decision

Final tasks, live interaction versus recorded review, which conditions to compare, participant population, primary outcomes, and the study protocol. Hardware and adaptive AI-generated interfaces are possible later directions, not implemented here.

How we could evaluate it

Separate perceived output quality, process quality, output control, and process control. Pair ratings with observable judgments, such as whether participants detect a pricing mismatch or identify an unperformed check. Free exploration and assigned conditions answer different questions. No study or validation claim is made by this prototype.
